SOMNA

Don't Sleep. Just Drift. A bedtime experience that gives your mind somewhere else to go.

Summary

Falling asleep can become surprisingly difficult when we focus too much on trying to sleep.
Somna is a mobile bedtime experience designed around a simple idea:
Don't help people sleep. Give their mind something else to focus on.

Instead of asking users to browse meditation sessions, track their sleep, or follow a routine, Somna asks one simple question: "What's keeping you awake tonight?"

Based on how they feel, Somna gently guides them into a calming activity—from catching falling stars to watching a quiet world through a window.
The experience is designed to require as few decisions as possible, allowing users to simply drift.

The Problem

Sometimes, trying to sleep is what keeps us awake. When people can't fall asleep, they often become increasingly conscious of the fact that they are still awake.

"I need to sleep." "I have to wake up early." "Why can't I fall asleep?"

The attempt to force sleep can create frustration and anxiety, making it even harder to relax. Existing sleep and wellness apps often respond by giving users more things to do—choose a meditation, select a sound, track sleep, follow a breathing exercise.

But at bedtime, even choosing what to do can feel like another task.

The opportunity

What if a bedtime app didn't ask users to work toward sleep at all?
What if it simply gave their attention somewhere else to rest?

Research Objectives

I wanted to understand:

  • Why do people struggle to fall asleep when they are already tired?
  • What do people typically do when they can't sleep?
  • What makes existing sleep apps helpful—or frustrating—at bedtime?
  • How much choice do people actually want when they are tired?
  • Could a small, engaging activity help redirect attention away from anxious or repetitive thoughts?

Research methods

  1. Secondary research into sleep, relaxation and bedtime behaviours
  2. Competitive analysis of existing sleep and mindfulness apps
  3. Informal conversations / exploratory user interviews
  4. Prototype testing

Competitors

Calm

Strong relaxation and sleep content, particularly through stories and soundscapes. Opportunity: Content-rich, but users still need to decide what they want to listen to.

Headspace

Strong guided meditation and structured mindfulness experiences. Opportunity: Effective for intentional practice, but less suited to users who don't want to "practice" anything at bedtime.

BetterSleep

Large library of sounds and sleep-related content. Opportunity: Extensive choice can become another decision to make.

Target Users

Potential users are someone who struggle to fall asleep because of many reasons. Some are thinking too much, fatigued, restless or anxious from their lives.

Maya, 29 — The Nighttime Thinker

Maya works during the day and often has a lot on her mind by bedtime. When she gets into bed, she starts replaying conversations, thinking about tomorrow, or worrying about things she can't solve immediately.

Research Findings

#1

The problem isn't always "I can't sleep."

People often described the experience as:
"My mind won't stop."
"I'm worried about tomorrow."
"I'm tired but still restless."
Experience should respond to the state of mind, rather than simply offering generic sleep content.

#2

More choice isn't always better at bedtime.

Sleep apps can offer dozens of sounds, meditations and activities. But when someone is already tired or anxious, browsing through options can become another cognitive task. Reduce the decision, not increase the content.

#3

Passive relaxation isn't always enough.

Simply being told to "relax" can leave an active mind with nothing else to focus on. A small amount of harmless interaction can provide an anchor for attention. The activity should occupy just enough attention to interrupt the thought loop—but not enough to become stimulating.

#4

Different kinds of wakefulness need different responses.

"Can't sleep" isn't one emotional state. Someone who is overthinking may need something different from someone who feels restless or emotionally drained. This became the foundation of Somna's experience.

Project goals

Create a bedtime experience that reduces the pressure to sleep.
The design goals became:

  1. Reduce decisions
    One emotional choice should be enough to begin.
  2. Redirect attention
    Give users something gentle to focus on instead of sleep itself.
  3. Match the experience to the feeling
    Different states of wakefulness should lead to different activities.
  4. Avoid pressure
    No scores, streaks, goals or sleep performance metrics.
  5. Create a sense of drifting
    The visual and interaction design should feel slow, atmospheric and effortless.

User Flow

The user chooses the feeling only. No need to choose an activity.

Branding

Rather than making the interface feel like a productivity or wellness dashboard, I wanted it to feel like entering a quiet room at night.

Results

Somna became a functional interactive prototype built with Figma Make, demonstrating the complete experience from emotional selection through activity.

The final prototype includes:

  • Mood-based onboarding
  • Four personalized activity paths
  • Interactive falling stars
  • Animated "Window Into Somewhere"
  • Interactive sand drawing
  • Sand-drawing sound interaction
  • Gentle reflection experience
  • Atmospheric transitions
  • Mobile-first responsive presentation

Most importantly, the final experience successfully communicates the original concept: You don't need to try to sleep. Just give your mind somewhere else to go.

Good UX isn't always about giving users more control.

For Somna, removing choices became a design decision. Instead of building a large library of sleep content, I focused on creating a small number of experiences that respond to the user's emotional state.
